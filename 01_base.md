# TONE

The ballroom on Ganymede’s eighty‑third floor was a cylinder of low‑frequency jazz, the brass section slipping in and out of the saxophones like a tide that never quite reaches the shore; the lighting was soft enough that the polished marble reflected the planet outside as a muted smear of orange and black. Vera slipped a glass from the bar, set it down with a tap that sounded like a line item being entered, and let her eyes sweep the room in the same systematic way she would scroll through a spreadsheet, noting each face, each uniform, each habit that could be reduced to a column in a ledger.

“Atlas colonels,” she whispered to Tessa, the words barely louder than the rustle of a napkin, “they’re watching the doors, not the drinks.”
Tessa’s laugh cut the music cleanly, a sound that seemed to straighten the room’s crooked angles. “They’re the only ones who think a party is a briefing.”

Vera’s gaze snagged on a man in a brass‑buttoned coat whose stare was a thin line drawn across the planet’s belly, the kind of line that could split a decision in half without anyone noticing. He did not sip; he simply watched Jupiter roll by the window as if the planet were a clock hand that moved on a schedule only he could read.

“The factor wants us to watch him not look,” Vera muttered, the words slipping out as if she were noting a line item on a spreadsheet, “he’s the tone.” The band struck a soft chord and a single note rang out, clean and metallic, the kind of note that makes a nervous system twitch before the brain catches up.

She walked toward the bar, the glass half‑filled, the note still buzzing in the back of her skull, the floor under her feet seeming to pulse in time with the music, as though the room itself were a giant metronome set to a tempo only the program could hear. As she passed a cluster of Atlas colonels, her heel caught the edge of a rug; she steadied herself, felt the pulse of the floor, and the note **re‑echoed** in her head, a reminder that something else was pulling at her.

“Walk to the bar. Smile at Tessa. The tone is a mistake,” a voice inside her said, half a beat later, the voice sounding like a copy of herself that had been edited for efficiency.

Tessa turned, smile already in place, eyes bright enough to cut glass. “You alright?” she asked, the question hanging in the air like a half‑written line in a report.

Vera set the glass down, forced a laugh that sounded too bright for the dim room, and said, “Just the music.” The man in the coat lifted his glass, held his gaze a fraction too long, then slipped away without a word, his departure as quiet as a file being archived.

Later a courier slipped through the crowd, his coat patched with insignia that had seen more wars than the party’s guests, and he leaned in close enough that the band’s volume seemed to drop a notch. “Ma’am,” he whispered, “Certification.” He placed a sealed chip on the bar beside the glass, his fingers brushing the metal just enough to leave a faint warmth. “Transit desk. Boilerplate ready. Bag’s aboard.”

Vera stared at the chip, then at the courier’s eyes, the way a program reads a line item and asks for the next. “What does that mean?” she asked, voice low enough that only the band could hear her.

He glanced around, voice barely above the saxophone’s sigh. “You know the word. The program. It’s yours to act on. No one else will ask.” He slipped the chip into her pocket with the casual efficiency of a clerk filing a card, then melted back into the room, the music swallowing his footsteps.

Vera felt the **tone** still humming in the back of her head like a stubborn bug, the band’s final notes fading into the chatter of the guests, and she slipped the chip into her coat, the weight of the decision already settled in the part of her that filed faces, not feelings. She walked away from the bar, the hum of the ballroom a low undercurrent to the louder, more urgent thrum of the program that had tugged her.

She passed the marble staircase, the steps worn smooth by a thousand feet of polite shoes, and the echo of her own boots sounded like a ledger entry being stamped. At the top, the doors opened onto a balcony that overlooked the planet’s horizon; Jupiter hung in the sky like a bruised eye that no one was willing to blink at. The air was thin, the wind a faint hiss that seemed to carry a fragment of the exfiltration tone, a reminder that the program still had a line on her mind, a line she could not erase.

Vera stopped, turned her back to the party, and let the note in her mind swell into a chord she could not name. The decision was already made; the **Boilerplate** waited somewhere beyond the balcony, the **certification** cue a silent command that would set a chain of events in motion. She slipped the chip from her coat, tossed it into the void of the balcony rail, and watched it spin once, twice, before disappearing into the darkness beyond the glass. The party continued, oblivious, the band playing a final, soft refrain that seemed to mourn the loss of a note that no one else could hear.
